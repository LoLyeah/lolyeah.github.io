INDUSTRIAL CASE #1

The Manpower Law Dilemma: UU 13/2003, The Severance Deficit & Omnibus Law Reforms

[Community OP & Commenters Debate]
“Megawati's labor law was like price control: intended to give workers more rights, but in reality, foreign factory investors were spooked, pushing millions of workers into the unregulated informal sector without rights or pensions.”

Empirical Fact Check: Substantially Supported by Labor Studies (SMERU 2007, World Bank 2014, LPEM FEB UI). Law No. 13/2003 established one of the highest statutory severance pay multipliers in the developing world (up to 32 months of salary for long-tenure dismissal). However, because employers had no pre-funded escrow accounts, over 66% of laid-off formal workers never received statutory severance upon company liquidation.

This created an unintended equilibrium: firms avoided formal permanent contracts, relying heavily on rolling contract labor (*PKWT*) and outsourcing, while labor-intensive garment/footwear FDI shifted to Vietnam, Cambodia, and Bangladesh. The 2020 Omnibus Law (UU Cipta Kerja / UU No. 6/2023) attempted to rebalance this by capping severance at 25 months and introducing the state-backed Job Loss Insurance (Jaminan Kehilangan Pekerjaan / JKP) funded via BPJS Ketenagakerjaan.

INDUSTRIAL CASE #2

Vietnam's Samsung Playbook: High-Tech Export Surge vs Local Value Retention Limits

[Community Contributor — Global Trade Analysis]
“Vietnam captured the entire electronics assembly segment because of 15–30 year tax holidays, bilateral FTAs with the EU/US, and fast port logistics. But 80% of Vietnam's exports and 95% of its electronics are controlled by foreign MNCs with low domestic supply chain capture.”

Empirical Fact Check: Verified by UN Comtrade & General Statistics Office of Vietnam. In 2008, Samsung committed its first $670 million factory in Bac Ninh, expanding over 15 years to over $22.4 Billion USD in cumulative investment across 6 factories, manufacturing over 50% of Samsung's global smartphones.

Vietnam achieved this through aggressive corporate tax holidays (0% for 4 years, 5% for 9 years, 10% for 15 years), dedicated power lines, proximity to Shenzhen semiconductor clusters, and the EU-Vietnam Free Trade Agreement (EVFTA). However, the community point is equally valid: over 70% of Vietnam's total export value and 95% of electronics exports are generated by Foreign Invested Enterprises (FIEs). Domestic Vietnamese suppliers are largely confined to packaging and simple plastics, capturing only 15–20% of value add.

INDUSTRIAL CASE #3

The Philippines $38B BPO Engine: Can Services Substitute Manufacturing?

[Community Contributor — Services & Education]
“Manufacturing is not the only path. The BPO (Business Process Outsourcing) industry in the Philippines accounts for 9% of GDP and employs 1.7 million high-earning middle-class workers due to high English proficiency, which Indonesia completely failed to tap.”

Empirical Fact Check: Confirmed. The Philippine IT-BPM (Information Technology and Business Process Management) sector generated $35.5 Billion USD in revenue in 2023 (~8.5% of GDP) and directly employs 1.7 million middle-class professionals. The Philippines leveraged its American colonial heritage, neutral accent, and high English proficiency (EF EPI Rank #20 worldwide) to capture global customer care, medical billing, accounting, and software development.

By contrast, Indonesia ranks #79 on the EF English Proficiency Index (Low Proficiency). Indonesian service exports are overwhelmingly dominated by inbound tourism (Bali), while high-value knowledge services remain nascent. However, development economists note that BPO alone cannot absorb tens of millions of low-skill vocational school graduates without a massive labor-intensive manufacturing backbone.

INDUSTRIAL CASE #4

The ICE vs EV Dilemma: 2,000 Moving Parts vs 20 Parts Threatening 1.5M Auto SME Workers

[Community OP — Automotive Ecosystem]
“Betting entirely on EVs will kill Indonesia's automotive supplier network. ICE cars have thousands of moving parts requiring a massive SME parts ecosystem in Karawang and Bekasi; EVs have far fewer moving parts, threatening hundreds of thousands of supplier jobs.”

Empirical Fact Check: Engineering and Employment Data Confirmed. An Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) powertrain and transmission assembly contains over 2,000 precision moving parts (pistons, fuel injectors, exhaust systems, camshafts, radiators, catalytic converters, multi-speed transmissions). According to the Indonesian Automotive Parts and Components Association (GIAMM), this supports approximately 1.5 million direct and indirect workers across Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 SME machine shops concentrated in West Java.

A Battery Electric Vehicle (BEV) powertrain contains only 20 to 50 moving parts (electric motor rotor, single-speed reduction gear, inverter). Over 40% of a BEV's bill of materials is concentrated in the lithium/nickel battery pack produced in automated mega-factories (e.g. HLI Karawang). Without a deliberate bridge strategy—such as Hybrid Electric Vehicles (HEVs) which retain the full ICE supplier ecosystem while reducing fuel consumption—a forced rapid transition to pure BEVs risks hollowing out domestic parts SMEs.

INDUSTRIAL CASE #5

Thailand's Warning: The 'Detroit of the East' That Aged Out of High-Income Status

[Community Contributor — Regional Demographics]
“Indonesia is not too late yet, but Thailand is. Thailand failed to develop its own domestic automotive brands, remained an assembly hub for Japanese MNCs, and its population aged rapidly before its income crossed $8,000.”

Empirical Fact Check: Highly Accurate. Thailand built Southeast Asia's largest automotive export hub ('Detroit of the East'), producing over 2 million vehicles annually. However, Thailand reached a GDP per capita plateau of ~$7,200–$7,800. It failed to transition from assembly (1i/2i) to domestic R&D/brand ownership (3i).

Critically, Thailand's Total Fertility Rate (TFR) collapsed to 1.16 births per woman in 2023, and over 20% of its population is now aged 60+ (Super-Aged Society). With domestic consumption contracting and Chinese EV makers disrupting traditional Japanese supply chains in Rayong, Thailand is the premier Southeast Asian example of a nation becoming old before getting rich.
